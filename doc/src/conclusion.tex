\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

For a small step-controlled CGRA behind a serial link, communication, not
the fabric, determines the cost of every kernel studied. Exact counts and a
first-order model answer the three questions within that scope. Fusion
reduces transactions by $1.99$--$3.95\times$; it is never slower for kernels
whose read-back it shrinks, and for the others it pays only once the
per-transaction turnaround exceeds between two thirds of a byte time and two
byte times. Geometry
changes traffic by up to an order of magnitude while useful work per PE slot
stays at $1/16$ for three of five kernels, so static occupancy is a poor
proxy for efficiency. Resident operands remove most of the traffic through
batching alone ($4.6\times$) and more through reuse, at a substantial cost
in flip-flops and in verification effort.

Methodologically, the study shows that the premise of a multicycle timing
exception can be proved on the production RTL and connected, by named
registers, to the exceptions that STA checks; the remaining gap is the
correspondence between RTL and netlist registers. The decisive missing
evidence is physical: measured turnaround distributions, call times and a
CPU baseline on a programmed board would turn the break-even analysis into
a measured result.
